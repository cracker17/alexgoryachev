The short version

A practitioner, not a futurist.

Alex Goryachev is an AI keynote speaker, strategic advisor, and Wall Street Journal-bestselling author based on the West Coast of the USA — a LinkedIn Top AI Voice who spent nearly two decades at Cisco as Managing Director of Innovation Strategy, running a $1.1B innovation portfolio and building innovation centers across 14 countries.

Since 2022 he's been Innovator-in-Residence at Tulane University, and since 2025 he's advised the California State University system on AI adoption and governance. In the past 12 months alone, Alex has delivered agentic AI keynotes across 11 countries.

The core conviction

Every organization that fails at transformation fails the same way: they invest in the tools, and forget to invest in the people who have to use them.

One belief, every wave

Tested across every wave of disruption.

Keep scrolling through three decades of watching technology threaten people and free them in the same breath.

01 My starting point

Both truths at once.

AI is the most incredible tool we have ever built, and the one most capable of leaving people behind. The fear is real. So is the hope. The entire job is making sure hope wins — and that only happens when we use these tools responsibly, deliberately, and in service of the humans who have to live with them.

02 The early internet years

Napster, Liquid Audio, IBM & Pfizer.

An early pioneer of the commercial internet: part of Napster, the platform that upended the entire music business, and before that Liquid Audio, where algorithmic distribution and behavioral data were the product. One lesson held: disruption rewards the people who stay close to the humans, not just the technology.

03 Scaling innovation at Cisco

$1.1B, 14 countries, 72,000 people.

Built a global innovation function from the ground up — co-innovation centers on five continents and innovation programs for three Olympic Games. The lesson that stuck: the programs that worked were the ones that made ordinary employees feel their ideas mattered.

04 The work that matters most now

Education & reskilling.

The single most important job of this century. Alex works with the California State University system — the largest public university system in the U.S. — on its AI Insights strategy, and serves as Innovator-in-Residence at Tulane and a Fellow of the Royal Society of Arts.

05 In the room today

Google, AWS, Bosch, Dell, Amgen.

The same thinking carried into rooms for Google, AWS, Bosch, Coca-Cola, and Rotary International — and into advisory for Dell, building a world-class AI consultancy, and Amgen, on early AI pilots. The tools keep changing. The conviction never does.

The framework

Three moves to stay above the algorithm.

A model Alex brings to every organization he works with, from Fortune 50 boards to the largest public university system in the country. As AI gets better at the work, one position stays durable: keeping people above the algorithm, not beneath it.

Move one

Adapt or vanish

The tech cycle now moves faster than the org chart. Build the muscle to change continuously, with measured experiments, fast learning, and real outcomes, so you lead the pace instead of chasing it.

1
Move two

Invest in what only people have

Judgment, trust, creativity, and taste are the durable advantage. Develop the human capabilities AI amplifies rather than replaces, and point your people squarely at them.

2
Move three

Democratize the tools

Value compounds when the whole workforce can build with AI. Hand everyone a maker space, spread the skills wide, and let the best ideas come from anywhere in the organization.

Point of view

Things I believe about AI and innovation.

Nine convictions that show up, in one form or another, in every keynote, workshop and advisory room.

01

“Adapt or vanish” is a literal description of the timeline.

Most organizations are pricing in a slower AI timeline than the one actually arriving. The gap between the fast adapters and everyone else widens by the month.

02

Innovation is rarely born in corner offices.

The best ideas usually come from the people closest to the problem, not the people highest on the org chart.

03

Education and reskilling are the defining work of our era.

The highest-leverage investment any leader or country can make is in the skills of their people. Get that right and almost everything else follows.

04

It is not the future that kills great companies. It is loyalty to the past.

The most dangerous phrase in any boardroom is “this is how we have always done it.”

05

Automation is wonderful, until you automate the one person who understood how anything worked.

The org chart never shows where the real knowledge lives. Automate without understanding it, and you scale the gaps alongside the gains.

06

Get above the algorithm, or get automated.

The real advantage belongs to those who keep direction, judgment, and the final call human — and treat AI as leverage instead of a leash.

07

Hiring a Chief AI Officer to “handle the AI” is like hiring a Chief Electricity Officer in 1920.

AI is not a department you can delegate to. It is the new wiring in the whole building.

08

Your employees are already using AI. The only question is whether they will tell you.

A ban just costs visibility. A sanctioned path that ships faster than the workaround gets both control and real adoption.

09

Half of what gets sold as an “AI strategy” is a slide deck with a robot on it.

Real strategy survives contact with a P&L. If your plan changes nothing about how work gets done on Monday, it is decoration, not direction.
